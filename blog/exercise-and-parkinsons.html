Exercise and Parkinson's: Why Movement Matters

For people living with Parkinson's disease, regular, purposeful movement is one of the most powerful tools available — and research continues to strengthen the case.

Parkinson's disease is a progressive movement disorder, and it can make the idea of exercise feel intimidating. Yet regular physical activity is widely recognized as an essential part of living well with Parkinson's — not a replacement for medical care, but a valuable complement to it.

Why movement helps

Exercise supports balance, flexibility, strength, and gait — the very areas Parkinson's tends to affect. Staying active can help people maintain independence in daily tasks, reduce the risk of falls, and support mood and overall wellbeing. Many people also find that movement helps them feel more in control of their bodies and their day.

The kinds of movement that help most

Programs for Parkinson's often emphasize big, intentional movements, rhythm, balance work, and strength. Activities like large-amplitude exercise, boxing-style drills, dancing, and stretching are popular because they target the specific challenges of the condition. The best program is one that is safe, enjoyable, and consistent.

Consistency matters more than intensity. Regular, moderate movement done safely, week after week, is what makes the difference.

Starting safely

Everyone's experience of Parkinson's is different, so programming should be tailored and supervised, especially at the start. Working with trained professionals — and, ideally, in a supportive group — helps people move at the right level and progress safely over time. It also addresses something medication can't: the isolation that so often accompanies the condition.

Community is part of the medicine

Doing this alongside others who understand builds accountability, encouragement, and genuine connection. That's exactly why our Cooking with Exercise program — created in partnership with the Rochester Parkinson's Network — pairs adaptive movement and hands-on nutrition with real community, all in one welcoming session.

This article is for general education and isn't medical advice. Parkinson's affects everyone differently — talk with your doctor or care team before starting any new exercise program.
